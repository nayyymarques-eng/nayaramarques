An advisors platform for four business verticals

A workspace for advisors across BTG Pactual’s verticals: configurable dashboard, modular side menu, client page. Four directors could each veto it. I led it from 12 interviews to a concept test with 90% acceptance and one MVP all four signed.

Role

Product Design Lead.

Team

Me plus 4 product designers, with tech leads and 4 business directors.

Context

The investment verticals at one of Latin America's largest investment banks.

Contribution

research · concept design · concept testing · workshop facilitation · prioritisation · team leadership

01 — The advisors platform

Three business directors asked for it. Their advisors called the platform incomplete, with features scattered across tools, and it hurt their performance.

I ran Phase 1, research to concept. Promoted to Design Lead, I built the Phase 2 team: four designers, one module each, with me still designing.

Independent offices selling BTG products. Advisors inside the bank, facing clients directly. Advisors managing large fortunes, the bank's biggest specialty. A separate structure from an acquired bank.

Note · BTG Pactual

Two and a half years at BTG Pactual, in retail and investments. I shaped the early MVP of BTG+, the consumer app, now one of the group’s largest businesses. This project is on the investment side.

02 — The problem

Advisors were doing the integration work the software should have done. What the interviews found, across three of the four verticals.

I interviewed 12 advisors across B2B, B2C and Advisors. Wealth was left out of the first concept to keep scope honest.

The hypothesis held: the information behind each decision was split across Portal Admin, Excel, Notes, Research and Power BI.

  • Wrong information
  • Information not updated
  • No useful filters
  • No opportunity curation
  • Inefficient customer support
  • Too many channels
  • Incomplete client profile
  • Control outside the platform
  • Multitasking
  • Accessibility

03 — Constraints

One product had to serve four verticals, each with its own director and its own idea of what the first release should hold.

Each vertical defined the MVP differently, and each director could veto. Nobody owned definition and prioritisation, so I took it on. Hence the workshops. The concept had to read clearly to directors, not just designers.

04 — Process & key decisions

Two phases: Concept Design to win approval, then Detailed Design to make it buildable. Three principles ran through both: customizable, multitasking, propositional.

research → concept design → concept test → refinement → project definition → business approval
Me, with tech leads and business directorsdetailed design → usability test → refinement → design spec → handover → business approval
Me as design lead, plus 4 product designers
I covered the whole workday, then showed the board only the nine that carried the argument. No variant per vertical: each office configures its own widgets. One codebase, four workflows. Workshop 1: feature map and impact-versus-effort matrix with tech, business and design leads. Workshop 2: each vertical proposed an MVP; we merged them into one. My team set up an "Advisors + Design Group" of volunteer advisors. It became the research pool for all of BTG design.

05 — The solution

A configurable dashboard, a modular side menu, and a client page built as a workspace, with the advisor’s tools attached.

Six widget modules (Metas, Clientes, Produtos, Tickets, Movimentações, Agenda), reached through around 150 prototypes.

06 — Impact

Results from the concept test and the approval that followed. They are test scores, not results from a shipped product.

Results

  • 90%feature acceptance in the concept test, 9 advisors across 3 verticals
  • 85%acceptance for tabs, read as browser tabs
  • 4 → 1verticals aligned on one prioritised MVP

The board approved the concept. Two findings changed the build: the side menu needed text labels, and the client page was too sparse for people used to dense data.

07 — What I learned

Alignment is a design deliverable. Until four directors saw the same tradeoffs, no design was safe to build.

How I split the team mattered more than how I briefed it. Each designer owned a module, not a vertical, so every module served all four workflows and reviews stayed about the product. Scoring each screen, instead of asking for opinions, gave the board a number to trust and me findings I hadn't looked for.

08 — What this case doesn't cover

The case runs from the first interviews to board approval. What happened after that is not covered:

The 90% and 85% are concept-test scores, not shipped results. I left before usage could be measured. Wealth was left out of the first MVP and never researched here. The modular dashboard may not hold for it. The concept, structure and prioritisation are mine; the detailed work inside each module belongs to the four designers I led. Three modules were in build when I left. I can’t show what changed after handover.
