An advisors platform for four business verticals

A workspace for advisors across BTG Pactual’s verticals: configurable dashboard, modular side menu, client page. Four directors could each veto it. I led it from 12 interviews to a concept test with 90% acceptance and one MVP all four signed.

Role

Product Design Lead.

01 — The advisors platform

Three business directors asked for it. Their advisors called the platform incomplete, with features scattered across tools, and it hurt their performance.

I ran Phase 1, research to concept. Promoted to Design Lead, I built the Phase 2 team: four designers, one module each, with me still designing.

02 — The problem

Advisors were doing the integration work the software should have done.

I interviewed 12 advisors across B2B, B2C and Advisors. Wealth was left out of the first concept to keep scope honest.

The hypothesis held: the information behind each decision was split across Portal Admin, Excel, Notes, Research and Power BI.

Wrong information Information not updated No useful filters No opportunity curation Inefficient customer support Too many channels Incomplete client profile Control outside the platform Multitasking Accessibility

03 — Constraints

A

Four verticals, four directors, one product. Each vertical defined the MVP differently, and each director could veto.

B

No project manager at the start. Nobody owned definition and prioritisation, so I took it on. Hence the workshops.

C

Board approval gated every step. The concept had to read clearly to directors, not just designers.

04 — Process & key decisions

Two phases: Concept Design to win approval, then Detailed Design to make it buildable. Three principles ran through both: customizable, multitasking, propositional.

Phase 1 · Concept

research → concept design → concept test → refinement → project definition → business approval

Me, with tech leads and business directors

Phase 2 · Detailed

detailed design → usability test → refinement → design spec → handover → business approval

Me as design lead, plus 4 product designers

Decision 01

Design 21 concept features, present 9

I covered the whole workday, then showed the board only the nine that carried the argument.

Gained

A concept broad enough to test as a real workday, tight enough to approve in one meeting.

Traded away

Twelve features went unshown; some resurfaced later as surprises.

Decision 02

A modular dashboard instead of four products

No variant per vertical: each office configures its own widgets. One codebase, four workflows.

Gained

90% acceptance in the concept test, and one thing four directors could say yes to.

Traded away

Configurability is expensive to build and easy to get lost in. Complexity moved to engineering and first-run setup.

Decision 03

Run the prioritisation myself, as two workshops

Workshop 1: feature map and impact-versus-effort matrix with tech, business and design leads. Workshop 2: each vertical proposed an MVP; we merged them into one.

Gained

One agreed MVP, with tradeoffs settled in the room, not relitigated every sprint.

Traded away

Weeks of my design time went to product management.

Decision 04

Recruit advisors into the design process permanently

My team set up an "Advisors + Design Group" of volunteer advisors. It became the research pool for all of BTG design.

Gained

Bi-weekly usability tests with real advisors, no re-recruiting.

Traded away

A self-selecting group skews enthusiastic, so we recruited outside it for bigger decisions.

05 — The solution

A configurable dashboard, a modular side menu, and a client page built as a workspace, with the advisor’s tools attached.

Six widget modules (Metas, Clientes, Produtos, Tickets, Movimentações, Agenda), reached through around 150 prototypes.

06 — Impact

90%

feature acceptance in the concept test, 9 advisors across 3 verticals

85%

acceptance for tabs, read as browser tabs

4 → 1

verticals aligned on one prioritised MVP

The board approved the concept. Two findings changed the build: the side menu needed text labels, and the client page was too sparse for people used to dense data.

07 — What I learned

Alignment is a design deliverable. Until four directors saw the same tradeoffs, no design was safe to build.

How I split the team mattered more than how I briefed it. Each designer owned a module, not a vertical, so every module served all four workflows and reviews stayed about the product.

Scoring each screen, instead of asking for opinions, gave the board a number to trust and me findings I hadn't looked for.

08 — What this case doesn't cover

From interviews to board approval. Not covered:

01

Adoption and business outcomes. The 90% and 85% are concept-test scores, not shipped results. I left before usage could be measured.

02

The Wealth vertical. Wealth was left out of the first MVP and never researched here. The modular dashboard may not hold for it.

03

Module-level design. The concept, structure and prioritisation are mine; the detailed work inside each module belongs to the four designers I led.

04

What shipped against the spec. Three modules were in build when I left. I can’t show what changed after handover.
